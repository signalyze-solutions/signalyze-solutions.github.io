How to Calculate CAC and LTV Before Spending Another Dollar on Marketing

November 12, 2026 · 6 min read

Many business owners increase their marketing budget when growth slows, assuming more spend will produce more customers. Sometimes it does. But without first understanding what a customer costs to acquire and what that customer is worth over time, additional spend can accelerate a loss rather than a gain. Two numbers — customer acquisition cost (CAC) and lifetime value (LTV) — tell you whether your current growth model is working before you commit another dollar.

What Customer Acquisition Cost Actually Measures

CAC is the total cost required to bring one new paying customer through the door. The common mistake is counting only ad spend. A more accurate calculation includes every expense directly tied to acquiring customers: advertising, agency or contractor fees, sales team salaries and commissions, marketing tools, and a reasonable share of any overhead that exists solely to support acquisition. Add those costs for a given period, then divide by the number of new customers gained in that same period.

CAC = Total Acquisition Costs ÷ New Customers Acquired

If you spent $18,000 last quarter across paid ads, a part-time marketing contractor, and your CRM subscription, and you gained 60 new customers, your CAC is $300. That number on its own means very little. It only becomes meaningful when you hold it next to lifetime value.

How to Estimate Lifetime Value Without a Data Science Team

Lifetime value is the total net revenue a typical customer generates before they stop buying. You do not need sophisticated modeling to get a useful estimate. A straightforward approach uses three inputs you likely already track: average purchase value, average purchase frequency per year, and average customer lifespan in years.

LTV = Average Purchase Value × Purchases Per Year × Customer Lifespan

If a typical customer spends $150 per transaction, buys four times a year, and stays with you for three years, their LTV is $1,800. For a more conservative and financially honest figure, apply your gross margin percentage to that number. If your gross margin is 50 percent, the margin-adjusted LTV is $900. Using margin-adjusted LTV is important because revenue alone can make a customer relationship look more profitable than it actually is.

The ratio does not tell you whether to spend more. It tells you whether spending more makes sense given what you currently know.

Reading the CAC-to-LTV Ratio

Once you have both numbers, divide LTV by CAC to produce the ratio. Using the examples above, $900 LTV divided by $300 CAC gives a ratio of 3:1. This is a widely referenced benchmark: a ratio below 1:1 means you are spending more to acquire a customer than you will ever recover, which is an urgent problem. A ratio between 1:1 and 3:1 suggests the model works but leaves limited room for error or reinvestment. A ratio above 3:1 often signals room to grow spend — though it can also mean you are underinvesting and leaving growth on the table. Ratios above 5:1 occasionally indicate the market is underpenetrated, but they can also reflect artificially low spend that will not hold as competition increases.

Where the Numbers Break Down — and What to Do About It

These calculations depend on the accuracy of your inputs. Customer lifespan is the most difficult to estimate, particularly for newer businesses without enough historical data. If your company is under three years old, you may not yet know how long customers stay. In that case, use a conservative estimate — one to two years — and revisit the calculation every quarter as more data accumulates. Similarly, if your customer base includes very different buyer types, calculate CAC and LTV separately by segment. Blending a high-value enterprise client with a low-value one-time buyer into a single average produces a number that accurately describes neither.

Turning the Ratio Into a Decision

The ratio is most useful as a recurring check rather than a one-time calculation. Run it monthly or quarterly, segment it by acquisition channel if your data allows, and watch how it moves over time. A declining ratio is an early warning that acquisition costs are rising faster than customer value — often before the problem appears in your profit and loss statement. A consistently improving ratio, by contrast, is evidence that your growth model is becoming more efficient and that additional investment is more likely to compound than to drain.

None of this requires complex tools. A spreadsheet with clean, consistently recorded data is enough to get started. The discipline is not in the calculation — it is in pulling the same numbers, defined the same way, on a regular schedule so that the trend becomes visible. That visibility is what turns CAC and LTV from accounting concepts into genuine decision inputs.
